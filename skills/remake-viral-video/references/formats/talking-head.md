# Format: talking head / location explainer

**When this format applies**: the viral video is one person telling the camera about one thing
(knowledge, a location visit, a breakdown, an opinion). This is the best-validated format: all seven
cases in the test set belong to it, and it is the only format this Skill remakes.

> Host edition. The runner does first frames, grading, captions and B-roll layout; take only the
> writing rules from this file.

## 1. The core of this format is the content layer, not the picture

Every line of a talking-head viral video states a fact. Making every fact in a rewritten line one
the creator has really stated is **the single highest-yield step** in this format: sourced claims
went from 0-4 out of 7 up to 7/7 through 16/16 (seven cases; see the evidence table in
`../rewrite-and-sources.md`).

On this route the reference's lines are kept and only four slots are rewritten
(`../creator-into-scene.md` section 0.2), so the content layer is mostly the reference's; the
author's own claims, the summary and the sign-off are where the creator's facts go.

## 2. Beat mapping: the viral video's job -> the creator's way of saying it

Preserve each beat's **job**. This section deliberately contains no finished script lines and no
worked beat mapping: having read anyone else's answer for the same viral video spoils your own.
Method conclusions and measured numbers stay; creative answers do not.

Common beat types in talking-head and location-visit viral videos, and the job to preserve when a
rewrite slot falls on them:

| Common beat type | Job to preserve | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Dangerous / counter-intuitive opening assertion | Threat hook: the first line makes it impossible to scroll away | If you rewrite it, use an equally counter-intuitive **specific number or assertion** the creator has stated, not a vague opinion |
| "We have just walked into X" | Establishes location, establishes "I am here" | **The beat most often done wrong.** Never invent a place the creator has never been; on this route the picture is the reference's place, so the line stays the reference's or is dropped with a reason |
| "You can see X, there are N of them..." | Scale of the location plus number impact | Numbers stay as the reference wrote them in kept lines; never change the original's number |
| Consecutive enumeration (three- or four-part list) | Builds pressure through rhythm | Keep the items and their on-screen list; one emoji per spoken item when icons cannot be seen |
| "I am inside X right now" | Puts the camera in the most uncomfortable place | Kept as the reference's line unless it is the author's own claim |
| "which is why..." unfinished sentence | Closes on suspense without giving the answer | Preserve "no answer given"; do not add a summary sentence that fills it in |
| The author's signature ending | Claim / call to follow | Replace with the creator's own recurring sign-off, verbatim from their videos; do not write a new one |

**Hard constraints stated in seconds or rates** are relative to the speaker: one measured original
ran at 3.57 words per second and the creator at only 2.8, so the original's "each beat under 1
second" was physically impossible for that creator. Judge pace at the creator's own rate
(`creator.speech_rate_median` in the brief).

**The "energy gap" for a single-presenter talking head** is the narrator's level of certainty
against how routine the thing described is to the people living it (for example 90 against 10).
Keep the gap from the original; swap the people, keep the difference.

## 3. Duration

Duration is set by the voice-over and must stay within +30% of the viral video. Measured versions
ranged from +9% to +26%. Derive the word count from the creator's speech rate; see section 7 of
`../rewrite-and-sources.md`.

## 4. What the runner does after the gate (for context only)

Captions are placed by the creator's habit first, never over a face, hands or a prop with text on
it. Generated footage is graded toward the creator's own footage from the same kind of setting.
Face likeness is compared against the creator's own footage; absolute similarity values are not
comparable across creators, so never quote one as a quality claim.
